1. Necessary Conditions for Stability of Continuous-Time LTI Systems & The Order Threshold

"State the necessary conditions for stability of a linear continuous-time system. Derive these conditions from the factored form of the characteristic polynomial. Prove that these conditions are sufficient for 1st and 2nd order systems, but not sufficient for 3rd order systems with an explicit counterexample."
Part A • Statement of the Necessary Conditions

Let the characteristic equation of an $n^{\text{th}}$-order continuous-time linear time-invariant (LTI) system be given by:

$$q(s) = a_0 s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + a_2 s^{n-2} + \dots + a_{n-1} s + a_n = 0 \quad (a_0 > 0)$$

For all roots of $q(s) = 0$ to lie strictly within the open Left-Half of the $s$-plane (open LHP, $\text{Re}(s) < 0$), the following two conditions are strictly necessary:

  1. Identical Algebraic Sign: All coefficients $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n$ must possess the same algebraic sign. Since $a_0 > 0$, this requires $a_i > 0$ for all $i \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$.
  2. Completeness (No Missing Powers): None of the coefficients may vanish ($a_i \neq 0$ for all $i$). No intermediate powers of $s$ may be missing, and the constant coefficient $a_n$ must be strictly non-zero.
Part B • Derivation from Factored Form

By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, any real polynomial $q(s)$ can be factored over the complex field into $m$ real roots $-\sigma_k$ and $p$ pairs of complex-conjugate roots $-\sigma_l \pm j\omega_l$, where $m + 2p = n$:

$$q(s) = a_0 \prod_{k=1}^m (s + \sigma_k) \prod_{l=1}^p \left[(s + \sigma_l)^2 + \omega_l^2\right] = 0$$

For the system to be asymptotically stable, all roots must lie strictly in the open LHP, which requires that all real parts be strictly negative:

$$\sigma_k > 0 \quad \forall k \in \{1, 2, \dots, m\}, \qquad \sigma_l > 0 \quad \forall l \in \{1, 2, \dots, p\}$$

Let us examine the constituent elementary polynomial factors:

  • First-order real factor: $(s + \sigma_k)$ where $\sigma_k > 0$. Both coefficients ($1$ and $\sigma_k$) are strictly positive and real.
  • Second-order complex factor: Expanding the squared conjugate pair: $$(s + \sigma_l)^2 + \omega_l^2 = s^2 + 2\sigma_l s + (\sigma_l^2 + \omega_l^2)$$ Since $\sigma_l > 0$, the linear coefficient is $2\sigma_l > 0$. Because $\omega_l \in \mathbb{R}$, $\omega_l^2 \ge 0$, which guarantees that the constant term $\sigma_l^2 + \omega_l^2 > 0$. Every quadratic factor has strictly positive real coefficients with no missing powers.

The total characteristic polynomial $q(s)$ is formed by taking the continued product of these elementary first- and second-order factors, each possessing only strictly positive coefficients, scaled by $a_0 > 0$. Since multiplication and addition of strictly positive real quantities can never produce a negative number or cancel out to zero, every resulting coefficient $a_i$ must be strictly positive:

$$a_i > 0 \quad \forall i \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$$

Thus, all coefficients must exist and share the same positive sign. $\blacksquare$

Part C • Sufficiency Proof for First- and Second-Order Systems

We now prove that for $n = 1$ and $n = 2$, the necessary conditions are also sufficient:

1. First-Order System ($n = 1$):

$$q(s) = a_0 s + a_1 = 0 \implies s = -\frac{a_1}{a_0}$$

If $a_0 > 0$ and $a_1 > 0$, the single root is $s = -a_1/a_0 < 0$, which strictly resides in the open LHP. Hence, $a_i > 0$ is sufficient for $n = 1$. $\checkmark$

2. Second-Order System ($n = 2$):

$$q(s) = a_0 s^2 + a_1 s + a_2 = 0 \quad (a_0, a_1, a_2 > 0)$$

Using the standard quadratic formula:

$$s_{1,2} = \frac{-a_1 \pm \sqrt{a_1^2 - 4a_0 a_2}}{2a_0}$$
  • Case I (Complex conjugate roots, $a_1^2 - 4a_0 a_2 < 0$): The real part is: $$\text{Re}(s_{1,2}) = -\frac{a_1}{2a_0} < 0$$ Since $a_0, a_1 > 0$, the real part is strictly negative.
  • Case II (Real roots, $a_1^2 - 4a_0 a_2 \ge 0$): Since $a_0, a_2 > 0$, we have $4a_0 a_2 > 0$, which implies: $$\sqrt{a_1^2 - 4a_0 a_2} < \sqrt{a_1^2} = a_1$$ Consequently, both roots are negative: $$-a_1 + \sqrt{a_1^2 - 4a_0 a_2} < 0 \quad \text{and} \quad -a_1 - \sqrt{a_1^2 - 4a_0 a_2} < 0$$

Therefore, for $n = 2$, positive coefficients guarantee that all roots lie in the open LHP. The conditions are sufficient. $\checkmark$

Part D • Non-Sufficiency for Third-Order Systems (Classic Counterexample)

For $n \ge 3$, having all coefficients strictly positive is NOT sufficient to ensure stability. Consider the classic counterexample from Prof. MM\'s lectures and Jadavpur University examinations:

$$q(s) = s^3 + s^2 + 2s + 8 = 0$$

Direct Inspection: All coefficients are strictly positive ($a_0 = 1, a_1 = 1, a_2 = 2, a_3 = 8 > 0$) and no intermediate terms are missing. The necessary conditions are fully satisfied.

Now factor $q(s)$ by synthetic division or inspection:

$$(s + 2)(s^2 - s + 4) = 0$$

Evaluating the roots:

$$s_1 = -2$$ $$s_{2,3} = \frac{-(-1) \pm \sqrt{(-1)^2 - 4(1)(4)}}{2} = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 16}}{2} = \mathbf{+0.5 \pm j\frac{\sqrt{15}}{2}} \approx \mathbf{+0.5 \pm j1.9365}$$

Stability Verdict: The complex conjugate pair $s_{2,3} = +0.5 \pm j1.9365$ has a positive real part ($\text{Re}(s) = +0.5 > 0$), placing two roots in the Right-Half $s$-Plane (RHP). The system is UNSTABLE.

Hence, for $n \ge 3$, positive coefficients alone do not guarantee stability. $\blacksquare$

Examiner Trap & Routh Algebraic Criterion In Jadavpur University class tests, students frequently write that positive coefficients ensure stability for 3rd-order systems. Always point out that for a third-order polynomial $s^3 + a_1 s^2 + a_2 s + a_3 = 0$, the necessary and sufficient condition for stability is $a_1 a_2 > a_3$. In this counterexample, $a_1 a_2 = 1 \times 2 = 2 < a_3 = 8$, which immediately signals instability without factoring.

2. Two-Parameter Tuning for Sustained Oscillations & Complete Pole Verification

"A unity negative feedback control system has open-loop transfer function: $$G(s) = \frac{K(s + 1)}{s^3 + b s^2 + 3s + 1}, \quad H(s) = 1$$ It is known that the closed-loop system exhibits sustained oscillations with frequency $\omega = 2\text{ rad/sec}$.
(a) Determine the values of parameter $b$ and controller gain $K$.
(b) Check whether any other roots lie in the Right-Half $s$-Plane for these parameter values."
Step 1 • Closed-Loop Characteristic Equation

The closed-loop characteristic equation is obtained from $1 + G(s)H(s) = 0$:

$$1 + \frac{K(s + 1)}{s^3 + b s^2 + 3s + 1} = 0$$ $$s^3 + b s^2 + 3s + 1 + K(s + 1) = 0$$ $$\mathbf{q(s) = s^3 + b s^2 + (3 + K)s + (1 + K) = 0}$$
Step 2 • Construction of the Parametric Routh Array

Formulate the Routh array in terms of unknown design parameters $b$ and $K$:

Row Column 1 Column 2 Algebraic Expression / Role
$s^3$ $1$ $3 + K$ Coefficients $a_0 = 1, a_2 = 3 + K$
$s^2$ $b$ $1 + K$ Coefficients $a_1 = b, a_3 = 1 + K$ (Forms Auxiliary Eqn)
$s^1$ $\dfrac{b(3 + K) - (1 + K)}{b}$ $0$ Must vanish for sustained oscillations
$s^0$ $1 + K$ $0$ Constant term
Step 3 • Enforcing Marginal Stability Condition at $\omega = 2\text{ rad/s}$

For the closed-loop system to sustain steady, undamped sinusoidal oscillations of frequency $\omega = 2\text{ rad/s}$, a pair of complex-conjugate poles must lie exactly on the imaginary axis at $s = \pm j2$. In the Routh array, this requires an entire row of zeros at row $s^1$:

$$\frac{b(3 + K) - (1 + K)}{b} = 0 \implies b(3 + K) - (1 + K) = 0 \implies 3b + bK - 1 - K = 0 \quad \text{--- (Eq. 1)}$$

When row $s^1$ vanishes, the oscillatory roots are governed by the Auxiliary Equation $A(s) = 0$ formed from the preceding row $s^2$:

$$A(s) = b s^2 + (1 + K) = 0$$

Since the oscillation frequency is $\omega = 2\text{ rad/s}$, we substitute $s = j2 \implies s^2 = -4$:

$$b(-4) + (1 + K) = 0 \implies -4b + 1 + K = 0 \implies \mathbf{K = 4b - 1} \quad \text{--- (Eq. 2)}$$
Step 4 • Simultaneous Solution for $b$ and $K$

Substitute Eq. 2 ($K = 4b - 1$) into Eq. 1 ($b(3 + K) - (1 + K) = 0$):

$$b\left(3 + [4b - 1]\right) - \left(1 + [4b - 1]\right) = 0$$ $$b(4b + 2) - 4b = 0$$ $$4b^2 + 2b - 4b = 0 \implies 4b^2 - 2b = 0 \implies 2b(2b - 1) = 0$$

This yields two mathematical solutions:

  1. $b = 0$: Discarded. Setting $b = 0$ violates the necessary condition for stability ($a_1 > 0$) and yields $K = 4(0) - 1 = -1 < 0$.
  2. $2b - 1 = 0 \implies \mathbf{b = 0.5 = \frac{1}{2}}$.

Now substitute $b = 0.5$ back into Eq. 2 to find controller gain $K$:

$$\mathbf{K = 4(0.5) - 1 = 2 - 1 = 1}$$

Thus, the required tuning parameters are: $b = 0.5$ and $K = 1$.

Step 5 • Part (b): Verification of Remaining Roots in $s$-Plane

Substitute $b = 0.5$ and $K = 1$ into the closed-loop characteristic equation:

$$q(s) = s^3 + 0.5 s^2 + (3 + 1)s + (1 + 1) = s^3 + 0.5 s^2 + 4s + 2 = 0$$

Multiply throughout by 2 to work with integers:

$$2s^3 + s^2 + 8s + 4 = 0$$

Factor by grouping:

$$s^2(2s + 1) + 4(2s + 1) = 0 \implies (2s + 1)(s^2 + 4) = 0$$

The closed-loop poles are:

$$s_1 = -0.5 = -\frac{1}{2} \quad \text{(Real root in open Left-Half Plane)}$$ $$s_{2,3} = \pm j2 \quad \text{(Simple conjugate pair on the imaginary axis)}$$

Conclusion for (b): Exactly ZERO roots lie in the Right-Half $s$-Plane. The remaining non-oscillatory pole lies at $s = -0.5$ in the Left-Half Plane. The system is strictly marginally stable! $\blacksquare$

Frequency-Domain Cross-Check (Prof. MM Recommended Verification) Substitute $s = j2$ directly into the loop transmission $G(s)H(s) = -1$: $$G(j2) = \frac{K(j2 + 1)}{(j2)^3 + b(j2)^2 + 3(j2) + 1} = \frac{K(1 + j2)}{-j8 - 4b + j6 + 1} = \frac{K(1 + j2)}{(1 - 4b) - j2} = -1$$ Equating numerator to negative denominator: $$K + j2K = -(1 - 4b) + j2 \implies 2K = 2 \implies K = 1$$ $$1 = -(1 - 4b) = 4b - 1 \implies 4b = 2 \implies b = 0.5$$ This two-line frequency-domain calculation confirms the Routh result with 100% precision!

3. Routh Stability Analysis & Precise Root Distribution for a Fourth-Order System

"Using Routh's stability criterion, determine the stability of a system having characteristic equation: $$s^4 + 2s^3 + s^2 + 4s + 2 = 0$$ Find the number of roots in the Left-Half, Right-Half, and on the imaginary axis of the $s$-plane."
Step 1 • Necessary Conditions Inspection

The characteristic equation is:

$$q(s) = s^4 + 2s^3 + s^2 + 4s + 2 = 0$$

Coefficients: $a_0 = 1, a_1 = 2, a_2 = 1, a_3 = 4, a_4 = 2$. All coefficients are strictly positive ($a_i > 0$) and no intermediate terms are missing. The necessary conditions are satisfied.

Step 2 • Formation of the Complete Routh Array

Construct the array using the standard determinant algorithm:

Row Col 1 Col 2 Col 3 Explicit Arithmetic Steps
$s^4$ $1$ $1$ $2$ Coefficients $a_0 = 1, a_2 = 1, a_4 = 2$
$s^3$ $2$ $4$ $0$ Coefficients $a_1 = 2, a_3 = 4$
$s^2$ $-1$ $2$ $0$ $b_1 = \dfrac{2(1) - 1(4)}{2} = \dfrac{-2}{2} = -1, \quad b_2 = \dfrac{2(2) - 1(0)}{2} = 2$
$s^1$ $8$ $0$ $0$ $c_1 = \dfrac{-1(4) - 2(2)}{-1} = \dfrac{-4 - 4}{-1} = \dfrac{-8}{-1} = 8$
$s^0$ $2$ $0$ $0$ $d_1 = \dfrac{8(2) - (-1)(0)}{8} = \dfrac{16}{8} = 2$
Step 3 • Analysis of First Column Sign Changes

Listing the elements of the first column of the Routh array in sequence:

$$r_{s^4} = +1, \quad r_{s^3} = +2, \quad r_{s^2} = -1, \quad r_{s^1} = +8, \quad r_{s^0} = +2$$

Examining sign transitions:

  1. $+1 \to +2$: No change (Positive to Positive).
  2. $+2 \to -1$: 1st sign change (Positive to Negative).
  3. $-1 \to +8$: 2nd sign change (Negative to Positive).
  4. $+8 \to +2$: No change (Positive to Positive).

There are exactly 2 sign changes in the first column.

Step 4 • Root Distribution on the $s$-Plane & Stability Verdict
  • Right-Half Plane Roots ($N_{\text{RHP}}$): Equal to the number of sign changes in the first column $\implies \mathbf{N_{\text{RHP}} = 2}$.
  • Imaginary Axis Roots ($N_{j\omega}$): Since no row of all zeros appeared in the array, there are no roots on the $j\omega$-axis $\implies \mathbf{N_{j\omega} = 0}$.
  • Left-Half Plane Roots ($N_{\text{LHP}}$): The polynomial is of degree $n = 4$: $$N_{\text{LHP}} = n - N_{\text{RHP}} - N_{j\omega} = 4 - 2 - 0 = \mathbf{2}$$

Final Verdict: The system possesses two poles in the Right-Half $s$-Plane and is therefore UNSTABLE. $\blacksquare$

Row Simplification Alternative • Didactic Note Students may simplify row $s^3$ by dividing all elements by 2, yielding $[1, 2, 0]$. Then: $$b_1 = \frac{1(1) - 1(2)}{1} = -1, \quad b_2 = \frac{1(2) - 0}{1} = 2$$ $$c_1 = \frac{-1(2) - 1(2)}{-1} = \frac{-4}{-1} = 4$$ The first column becomes $[1, 1, -1, 4, 2]$. Notice that while numerical magnitudes differ, the signs ($+, +, -, +, +$) and sign change count (2) remain completely identical!

4. The Hurwitz Determinant Criterion, Fourth-Order Minors & Algebraic Equivalence to Routh

"(a) State the Hurwitz criterion for stability of continuous-time linear systems.
(b) Construct the Hurwitz matrix $H$ and derive the expressions for principal minors $\Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3, \Delta_4$ for a general 4th-order characteristic polynomial.
(c) Derive the mathematical relationship between the elements of the first column of the Routh array and the Hurwitz determinants."
Part A • Statement of the Hurwitz Stability Criterion

Consider the $n^{\text{th}}$-order characteristic equation of an LTI system:

$$q(s) = a_0 s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + a_2 s^{n-2} + \dots + a_{n-1} s + a_n = 0 \quad (a_0 > 0)$$

Hurwitz Stability Theorem: For all roots of $q(s)$ to lie strictly in the open Left-Half $s$-Plane (asymptotic stability), it is necessary and sufficient that all $n$ principal leading diagonal minors $\Delta_1, \Delta_2, \dots, \Delta_n$ of the Hurwitz determinant $H_{n \times n}$ be strictly positive:

$$\Delta_k > 0 \quad \forall k \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$$
Part B • Construction of Hurwitz Matrix & Minors for $n = 4$

The square Hurwitz matrix $H$ is constructed by placing coefficients $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ along the main diagonal. Across each row, indices decrease by 1 moving left to right, and any coefficient with an index $< 0$ or $> n$ is replaced by zero:

$$H = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_0 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ a_3 & a_2 & a_1 & a_0 & \dots & 0 \\ a_5 & a_4 & a_3 & a_2 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \dots & a_n \end{bmatrix}_{n \times n}$$

For a fourth-order polynomial ($n = 4$, setting $a_5 = a_6 = a_7 = 0$), the $4 \times 4$ Hurwitz matrix is:

$$H_4 = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_0 & 0 & 0 \\ a_3 & a_2 & a_1 & a_0 \\ 0 & a_4 & a_3 & a_2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & a_4 \end{bmatrix}$$

The four principal leading diagonal minors are evaluated as follows:

  1. First Principal Minor: $$\mathbf{\Delta_1 = a_1 > 0}$$
  2. Second Principal Minor: $$\mathbf{\Delta_2 = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_0 \\ a_3 & a_2 \end{vmatrix} = a_1 a_2 - a_0 a_3 > 0}$$
  3. Third Principal Minor: $$\mathbf{\Delta_3 = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_0 & 0 \\ a_3 & a_2 & a_1 \\ 0 & a_4 & a_3 \end{vmatrix}}$$ Expanding along the first row: $$\Delta_3 = a_1 \begin{vmatrix} a_2 & a_1 \\ a_4 & a_3 \end{vmatrix} - a_0 \begin{vmatrix} a_3 & a_1 \\ 0 & a_3 \end{vmatrix} = a_1(a_2 a_3 - a_1 a_4) - a_0(a_3^2)$$ Factoring out $a_3$: $$\Delta_3 = a_3(a_1 a_2 - a_0 a_3) - a_1^2 a_4 = \mathbf{a_3 \Delta_2 - a_1^2 a_4 > 0}$$
  4. Fourth Principal Minor: Expanding along the fourth row or column of $H_4$: $$\mathbf{\Delta_4 = a_4 \Delta_3 > 0}$$

Since $a_4 > 0$ by the necessary condition, $\Delta_4 > 0$ is guaranteed whenever $\Delta_3 > 0$.

Part C • Numerical Demonstration (Prof. MM Slide Example • Nagrath & Gopal)

Consider $q(s) = s^4 + 8s^3 + 18s^2 + 16s + 5 = 0$ ($a_0 = 1, a_1 = 8, a_2 = 18, a_3 = 16, a_4 = 5$):

$$H = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 16 & 18 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 5 & 16 & 18 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$
  • $\Delta_1 = 8 > 0$
  • $\Delta_2 = 8(18) - 1(16) = 144 - 16 = 128 > 0$
  • $\Delta_3 = 16(128) - 8^2(5) = 2048 - 64(5) = 2048 - 320 = 1728 > 0$
  • $\Delta_4 = a_4 \Delta_3 = 5(1728) = 8640 > 0$

All principal minors are strictly positive $\implies$ The system is ASYMPTOTICALLY STABLE! $\checkmark$

Part D • Derivation of Relationship to Routh Array First Column (JU 2017 Q6a)

Formulate the general Routh array for $q(s) = a_0 s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + a_2 s^{n-2} + \dots$:

  • Row $s^n$: First element is $r_{1,1} = a_0$. (Define $\Delta_0 = a_0$).
  • Row $s^{n-1}$: First element is $r_{2,1} = a_1 = \Delta_1$.
  • Row $s^{n-2}$: First element is: $$r_{3,1} = b_1 = \frac{a_1 a_2 - a_0 a_3}{a_1} = \frac{\Delta_2}{\Delta_1}$$
  • Row $s^{n-3}$: First element is: $$r_{4,1} = c_1 = \frac{b_1 a_3 - a_1 b_2}{b_1} = \frac{\left(\frac{\Delta_2}{\Delta_1}\right) a_3 - a_1 a_4}{\frac{\Delta_2}{\Delta_1}} = \frac{a_3 \Delta_2 - a_1^2 a_4}{\Delta_2} = \frac{\Delta_3}{\Delta_2}$$

By mathematical induction, each first-column element is the ratio of two successive Hurwitz determinants:

$$\mathbf{r_{k, 1} = \frac{\Delta_{k-1}}{\Delta_{k-2}} \quad \text{for row index } k \ge 2}$$

Conclusion: All elements in the first column of the Routh array are positive if and only if all Hurwitz determinants are positive ($r_{k,1} > 0 \iff \Delta_k > 0$).

Computational Advantage of Routh: Evaluating an $n \times n$ determinant directly requires $\mathcal{O}(n!)$ arithmetic operations. Routh\'s triangular array condensation evaluates stability in $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ arithmetic steps! $\blacksquare$

JU Exam Grading Hallmark To secure full 8/8 marks on JU 2017 Q6(a), examiners mandate showing the algebraic substitution for $c_1$: explicitly proving that the numerator $\frac{\Delta_2}{\Delta_1} a_3 - a_1 a_4$ multiplies out to $a_3 \Delta_2 - a_1^2 a_4$, which matches the Laplace expansion of $\Delta_3$.

5. Parameter Stability Range, Critical Gain $K_{\text{mar}}$ & Sustained Oscillation Frequency

"The characteristic equation of a third-order feedback system is given by: $$s^3 + 3K s^2 + (K + 2)s + 4 = 0$$ (a) Determine the range of controller gain $K$ for which the closed-loop system is asymptotically stable.
(b) Find the critical gain $K_{\text{mar}}$ that drives the system to the verge of instability.
(c) Calculate the sustained frequency of oscillation $\omega_{\text{osc}}$ at this critical gain."
Step 1 • Necessary Conditions for Stability

The characteristic equation is:

$$q(s) = s^3 + 3K s^2 + (K + 2)s + 4 = 0$$

The polynomial coefficients are $a_0 = 1, a_1 = 3K, a_2 = K + 2, a_3 = 4$. For all coefficients to be strictly positive:

  1. $a_0 = 1 > 0$ (satisfied).
  2. $a_1 = 3K > 0 \implies K > 0$.
  3. $a_2 = K + 2 > 0 \implies K > -2$.
  4. $a_3 = 4 > 0$ (satisfied).

The combined necessary condition is $K > 0$.

Step 2 • Construct the Routh Array
Row Column 1 Column 2 Calculation Formula
$s^3$ $1$ $K + 2$ $a_0 = 1, a_2 = K + 2$
$s^2$ $3K$ $4$ $a_1 = 3K, a_3 = 4$
$s^1$ $\dfrac{3K(K + 2) - 1(4)}{3K} = \dfrac{3K^2 + 6K - 4}{3K}$ $0$ $\dfrac{a_1 a_2 - a_0 a_3}{a_1}$
$s^0$ $4$ $0$ Constant term $a_3 = 4$
Step 3 • Determine Range of $K$ for Asymptotic Stability

For asymptotic stability, all entries in the first column must be strictly positive:

  1. From row $s^2$: $3K > 0 \implies K > 0$.
  2. From row $s^1$: $\dfrac{3K^2 + 6K - 4}{3K} > 0$. Since $3K > 0$, this requires the quadratic numerator to be positive: $$3K^2 + 6K - 4 > 0$$

Solve the corresponding quadratic equality $3K^2 + 6K - 4 = 0$ using the quadratic formula:

$$K = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{6^2 - 4(3)(-4)}}{2(3)} = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{36 + 48}}{6} = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{84}}{6} = \frac{-6 \pm 2\sqrt{21}}{6} = -1 \pm \frac{\sqrt{21}}{3}$$

Evaluating numerically (with $\sqrt{21} \approx 4.582576$):

$$K_1 = -1 + \frac{4.582576}{3} = -1 + 1.527525 = \mathbf{+0.5275}$$ $$K_2 = -1 - 1.527525 = \mathbf{-2.5275}$$

Since the parabola $3K^2 + 6K - 4$ opens upward, it is positive for $K > K_1$ and $K < K_2$. Intersecting with the necessary condition $K > 0$, the allowable range of gain for asymptotic stability is:

$$\mathbf{K > \frac{\sqrt{21} - 3}{3} \approx 0.5275}$$
Step 4 • Critical Gain $K_{\text{mar}}$ & Frequency of Oscillation

The marginal critical gain that puts the system on the verge of instability is:

$$\mathbf{K_{\text{mar}} = \frac{\sqrt{21} - 3}{3} \approx 0.5275}$$

At $K = K_{\text{mar}}$, row $s^1$ vanishes. The auxiliary equation is formed from row $s^2$:

$$A(s) = 3K_{\text{mar}} s^2 + 4 = 0 \implies s^2 = -\frac{4}{3K_{\text{mar}}}$$

Substituting $s = j\omega_{\text{osc}} \implies s^2 = -\omega_{\text{osc}}^2$:

$$\omega_{\text{osc}}^2 = \frac{4}{3K_{\text{mar}}} = \frac{4}{3\left(\frac{\sqrt{21}-3}{3}\right)} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{21} - 3}$$

Rationalizing the denominator:

$$\omega_{\text{osc}}^2 = \frac{4(\sqrt{21} + 3)}{(\sqrt{21} - 3)(\sqrt{21} + 3)} = \frac{4(\sqrt{21} + 3)}{21 - 9} = \frac{4(\sqrt{21} + 3)}{12} = \frac{\sqrt{21} + 3}{3} = 1 + \frac{\sqrt{21}}{3}$$ $$\omega_{\text{osc}}^2 = 1 + 1.527525 = 2.527525\text{ rad}^2/\text{s}^2$$ $$\mathbf{\omega_{\text{osc}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{21} + 3}{3}} \approx 1.5898\text{ rad/sec}}$$
Analytical Insight • Two-Line Cartesian Verification Substitute $s = j\omega$ directly into $q(s) = 0$: $$(j\omega)^3 + 3K(j\omega)^2 + (K + 2)(j\omega) + 4 = 0 \implies (4 - 3K\omega^2) + j\omega(K + 2 - \omega^2) = 0$$ Equating real and imaginary parts to zero: $$\omega^2 = \frac{4}{3K} \quad \text{and} \quad \omega^2 = K + 2$$ Equating the two: $K + 2 = \frac{4}{3K} \implies 3K^2 + 6K - 4 = 0 \implies K_{\text{mar}} = 0.5275$. Then $\omega_{\text{osc}}^2 = K_{\text{mar}} + 2 = 0.5275 + 2 = 2.5275 \implies \omega_{\text{osc}} = 1.5898\text{ rad/s}$. Flawless match!

6. Special Case: Row of All Zeros, Auxiliary Equation & Multi-Frequency Oscillations

"Construct the Routh array for the system with characteristic equation: $$s^6 + 2s^5 + 8s^4 + 12s^3 + 20s^2 + 16s + 16 = 0$$ Using the auxiliary equation, determine the exact location of all roots of the polynomial and comment on system stability."
Step 1 • Initial Array Construction & Occurrence of All-Zero Row

Given the sixth-order polynomial:

$$q(s) = s^6 + 2s^5 + 8s^4 + 12s^3 + 20s^2 + 16s + 16 = 0$$

Construct the initial rows of the Routh array:

  • Row $s^6$: $[1, 8, 20, 16]$
  • Row $s^5$: $[2, 12, 16, 0]$. Dividing by positive scalar 2 yields $[1, 6, 8, 0]$.
  • Row $s^4$: $$b_1 = \frac{1(8) - 1(6)}{1} = 2, \quad b_2 = \frac{1(20) - 1(8)}{1} = 12, \quad b_3 = \frac{1(16) - 1(0)}{1} = 16$$ Dividing row $s^4$ by 2 yields $[1, 6, 8, 0]$.
  • Row $s^3$: $$c_1 = \frac{1(6) - 1(6)}{1} = 0, \quad c_2 = \frac{1(8) - 1(8)}{1} = 0, \quad c_3 = 0$$

Critical Breakdown: Row $s^3$ becomes an entire row of zeros. This indicates the presence of roots symmetrically distributed about the origin in the $s$-plane.

Step 2 • Formulation & Differentiation of Auxiliary Polynomial $A(s)$

The auxiliary polynomial $A(s)$ is constructed from the coefficients of the row immediately preceding the all-zero row (row $s^4$):

$$A(s) = 2s^4 + 12s^2 + 16 = 0 \iff s^4 + 6s^2 + 8 = 0$$

Differentiating $A(s)$ with respect to $s$:

$$\frac{dA(s)}{ds} = 4s^3 + 12s = 0$$

The coefficients of $\frac{dA(s)}{ds}$ ($4$ and $12$) replace the all-zero row $s^3$. Dividing by positive scalar 4 yields $[1, 3, 0]$.

$s^5$ $2$ (1) $12$ (6) $16$ (8) $0$ Divided by 2

Step 4 • Exact Analytical Root Locations

1. First-Column Sign Changes: The elements in the first column are $[1, 1, 1, 1, 3, 1/3, 8]$. All elements are strictly positive. There are 0 sign changes $\implies$ Zero roots in the Right-Half $s$-Plane.

2. Roots of the Auxiliary Polynomial $A(s) = 0$:

$$s^4 + 6s^2 + 8 = 0$$

Let $w = s^2$, transforming it into a quadratic equation in $w$:

$$w^2 + 6w + 8 = 0 \implies (w + 2)(w + 4) = 0 \implies w_1 = -2, \quad w_2 = -4$$

Substituting back $s^2 = w$:

$$s^2 = -2 \implies \mathbf{s_{1,2} = \pm j\sqrt{2}}$$ $$s^2 = -4 \implies \mathbf{s_{3,4} = \pm j2}$$

These constitute 4 non-repeated, purely imaginary roots on the $j\omega$-axis.

3. Remaining Roots from Quotient Polynomial $Q(s) = q(s) / A(s)$:

$$Q(s) = \frac{s^6 + 2s^5 + 8s^4 + 12s^3 + 20s^2 + 16s + 16}{s^4 + 6s^2 + 8} = s^2 + 2s + 2 = 0$$

Solving for the remaining two roots:

$$s_{5,6} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(2)}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-4}}{2} = \mathbf{-1 \pm j1}$$

Both remaining roots possess negative real parts ($\text{Re}(s) = -1 < 0$) and lie strictly in the open Left-Half Plane.

Step 5 • Root Distribution Summary & System Stability Verdict
  • Open Left-Half Plane: 2 roots ($s = -1 \pm j1$).
  • Imaginary Axis ($j\omega$-axis): 4 roots ($s = \pm j\sqrt{2}$ and $s = \pm j2$).
  • Right-Half Plane: 0 roots.

Verdict: Because all four roots on the imaginary axis are simple (non-repeated), the natural response exhibits sustained sinusoidal oscillations composed of two distinct fundamental angular frequencies:

$$\omega_1 = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\text{ rad/sec} \quad \text{and} \quad \omega_2 = 2.0\text{ rad/sec}$$

The system is MARGINALLY (LIMITEDLY) STABLE. $\blacksquare$

Examiner Warning • The Hazard of Repeated $j\omega$ Roots If the auxiliary equation had yielded repeated roots on the $j\omega$-axis (for instance, $(s^2 + 4)^2 = s^4 + 8s^2 + 16 = 0$), the inverse Laplace transform would contain terms of the form $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{\frac{1}{(s^2+4)^2}\} \propto t \sin(2t)$. The factor of $t$ causes the response amplitude to grow linearly toward infinity as $t \to \infty$, making the system UNSTABLE despite zero sign changes in the Routh array! Always emphasize this condition in university examinations.

7. Relative Stability, Settling Time Bound ($t_s \le 4/\sigma_0$) & Coordinate Transformation

"(a) Explain how Routh's criterion can be adapted to test the relative stability of a system.
(b) For a third-order system with characteristic equation: $$q(s) = s^3 + 7s^2 + 14s + 8 = 0$$ determine whether all closed-loop poles lie to the left of the vertical line $s = -1$ in the $s$-plane, thereby guaranteeing a settling time $t_s \le 4\text{ seconds}$ (2% tolerance band)."
Part A • Theoretical Basis of Relative Stability via Coordinate Shift

Absolute stability provides only a binary verdict: whether all closed-loop poles lie strictly in the Left-Half Plane ($\text{Re}(s) < 0$). However, if a dominant pole lies at $s = -0.001$, its transient exponential mode $e^{-0.001 t}$ decays so slowly that the system is practically unusable.

The speed of decay of transient response modes is dictated by the real part $\sigma$ of the dominant poles:

$$\text{Transient decay envelope} \propto e^{-\sigma t} \implies t_s \approx \frac{4}{\sigma} \quad \text{(2% criterion)}$$

To guarantee that every system mode decays at least as fast as $e^{-\sigma_0 t}$ (i.e. settling time $t_s \le \frac{4}{\sigma_0}$), all poles must lie to the left of a vertical line $s = -\sigma_0$. We define a coordinate shift:

$$\mathbf{s = z - \sigma_0} \quad (\sigma_0 > 0)$$

Substituting $s = z - \sigma_0$ shifts the imaginary axis from $\text{Re}(s) = 0$ to $\text{Re}(s) = -\sigma_0$. We then formulate the transformed polynomial:

$$\hat{q}(z) = q(z - \sigma_0) = 0$$

Applying Routh\'s stability criterion to $\hat{q}(z)$:

  • If all elements in the first column of the $z$-array are positive (zero sign changes), all roots of $\hat{q}(z)$ lie in $\text{Re}(z) < 0$.
  • Since $z = s + \sigma_0$, this rigorously implies $\text{Re}(s + \sigma_0) < 0 \implies \mathbf{\text{Re}(s) < -\sigma_0}$.
  • All poles lie strictly to the left of the vertical boundary $s = -\sigma_0$.
Part B • Coordinate Transformation for $q(s) = s^3 + 7s^2 + 14s + 8 = 0$

We are required to test whether all poles lie to the left of $s = -1$, corresponding to $\sigma_0 = 1$. Substitute $s = z - 1$ into $q(s)$:

$$\hat{q}(z) = (z - 1)^3 + 7(z - 1)^2 + 14(z - 1) + 8 = 0$$

Expanding each term via the binomial theorem:

  • $(z - 1)^3 = z^3 - 3z^2 + 3z - 1$
  • $7(z - 1)^2 = 7(z^2 - 2z + 1) = 7z^2 - 14z + 7$
  • $14(z - 1) = 14z - 14$
  • Constant term: $+8$

Collecting coefficients by descending powers of $z$:

  • $z^3$: $1$
  • $z^2$: $-3 + 7 = 4$
  • $z^1$: $3 - 14 + 14 = 3$
  • $z^0$: $-1 + 7 - 14 + 8 = 0$

The transformed characteristic equation is:

$$\mathbf{\hat{q}(z) = z^3 + 4z^2 + 3z + 0 = 0 \iff z(z^2 + 4z + 3) = z(z + 1)(z + 3) = 0}$$
Part C • Routh Array on Transformed Polynomial $\hat{q}(z)$

Construct the Routh array for $\hat{q}(z) = z^3 + 4z^2 + 3z + 0$:

Row Column 1 Column 2 Analytical Remarks
$z^3$ $1$ $3$ $a_0 = 1, a_2 = 3$
$z^2$ $4$ $0$ $a_1 = 4, a_3 = 0$
$z^1$ $3$ $0$ $\dfrac{4(3) - 1(0)}{4} = 3$
$z^0$ $0$ $0$ Row of zeros reveals root at $z = 0$

The zero element in row $z^0$ reveals that $\hat{q}(z)$ has a root located precisely on the boundary line $z = 0$.

The roots in the $z$-plane are: $z_1 = 0, \; z_2 = -1, \; z_3 = -3$.

Mapping back to the $s$-plane via $s = z - 1$:

$$\mathbf{s_1 = 0 - 1 = -1}$$ $$\mathbf{s_2 = -1 - 1 = -2}$$ $$\mathbf{s_3 = -3 - 1 = -4}$$
Part D • Engineering Evaluation & Settling Time Conclusion
  1. Boundary Location: The dominant pole $s_1 = -1$ lies precisely on the vertical boundary line $s = -1$. The remaining two poles ($s_2 = -2$ and $s_3 = -4$) lie strictly to the left of the line ($s < -1$).
  2. Transient Response Mode: The slowest decaying mode in the natural response is $e^{-1 \cdot t} = e^{-t}$.
  3. Settling Time Verification: For a 2% settling band: $$t_s = \frac{4}{\sigma_0} = \frac{4}{1} = 4\text{ seconds}$$

Conclusion: All closed-loop poles satisfy $\text{Re}(s) \le -1$. The settling time requirement $t_s \le 4\text{ seconds}$ is satisfied at the exact limit. $\blacksquare$

Synthetic Division (Horner\'s Scheme) Shortcut Instead of expanding binomial powers algebraically, repeatedly divide $q(s)$ by $(s + 1)$ using Horner\'s synthetic division: $$\begin{array}{r|rrrr} -1 & 1 & 7 & 14 & 8 \\ & & -1 & -6 & -8 \\ \hline & 1 & 6 & 8 & \mathbf{0} \\ -1 & & -1 & -5 & \\ \hline & 1 & 5 & \mathbf{3} & \\ -1 & & -1 & & \\ \hline & 1 & \mathbf{4} & & \end{array}$$ The bottom-up remainders $[1, 4, 3, 0]$ immediately give the coefficients of $\hat{q}(z) = z^3 + 4z^2 + 3z$. This saves 5 minutes in exam conditions!

8. BIBO vs. Asymptotic Stability: Internal Dynamics & Pole-Zero Cancellation Hazards

"(a) Distinguish clearly between BIBO stability and Asymptotic stability for linear time-invariant systems.
(b) Does BIBO stability necessarily imply asymptotic stability? Justify your answer with an explicit mathematical counterexample involving state-space formulation and pole-zero cancellation.
(c) State the necessary and sufficient conditions under which internal stability and external stability become equivalent."
Part A • Rigorous Comparative Distinction
Criteria / Dimension BIBO Stability (External / Input-Output) Asymptotic Stability (Internal / State Space)
Core Definition For every bounded input $|u(t)| \le M_u < \infty$, the output remains bounded $|y(t)| \le M_y < \infty$ for all $t \ge 0$, assuming zero initial stored energy. With zero input ($u(t) \equiv 0$), the internal state trajectory returns asymptotically to the equilibrium origin: $\lim_{t \to \infty} \|\mathbf{x}(t)\| = 0$ for all initial states $\mathbf{x}(0)$.
Mathematical Condition The impulse response $g(t)$ is absolutely integrable: $$\int_0^\infty |g(t)|\,dt < \infty$$ All transfer function poles lie in the open LHP. All eigenvalues of the system state matrix $\mathbf{A}$ (roots of $\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$) lie strictly in the open LHP: $\text{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$.
Observational Scope External input-output ports only. Internal hidden modes are completely invisible. Complete $n$-dimensional state vector $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Every physical energy storage element is tracked.
Response Component Governs the Zero-State Response: $$y_{zs}(t) = \int_0^t g(t - \tau) u(\tau)\,d\tau$$ Governs the Zero-Input Response: $$\mathbf{x}_{zi}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}(0)$$
Part B • Does BIBO Imply Asymptotic Stability? Explicit Counterexample

Answer: NO. In general, BIBO stability does NOT imply asymptotic stability.

If an internal subsystem possesses an unstable eigenvalue that is uncontrollable (uncoupled from the input) or unobservable (uncoupled from the output), pole-zero cancellation hides the unstable mode from the transfer function $G(s)$, making the system appear externally stable while internally exploding.

Explicit State-Space Counterexample:

Consider a second-order LTI system with state equations:

$$\dot{x}_1(t) = +1 \cdot x_1(t) + u(t) \quad \implies \text{Unstable Mode (Eigenvalue } \lambda_1 = +1 \in \text{RHP)}$$ $$\dot{x}_2(t) = -2 \cdot x_2(t) + u(t) \quad \implies \text{Stable Mode (Eigenvalue } \lambda_2 = -2 \in \text{LHP)}$$ $$y(t) = x_2(t) \quad \implies \text{Output sensor connected solely to } x_2$$

In standard vector-matrix state-space notation $\mathbf{\dot{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}u, \quad y = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}$:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Input-Output Transfer Function Analysis (External Stability):

$$G(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} s - 1 & 0 \\ 0 & s + 2 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{s - 1} \\ \frac{1}{s + 2} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{s + 2}}$$

Notice that the unstable pole at $s = +1$ does not appear in $G(s)$ because state $x_1$ has no transmission path to output $y(t)$ (it is unobservable). The transfer function has a single pole at $s = -2$ in the open LHP.

The impulse response is $g(t) = e^{-2t} u(t)$, which satisfies:

$$\int_0^\infty |g(t)|\,dt = \int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\,dt = \left[ -\frac{e^{-2t}}{2} \right]_0^\infty = \frac{1}{2} < \infty$$

Thus, the system is STRICTLY BIBO STABLE!

2. Zero-Input State Evolution (Internal Stability):

Now let the input be zero ($u(t) \equiv 0$), but assume an initial perturbation in state $x_1$: $\mathbf{x}(0) = [c, 0]^T$ with $c \neq 0$. The unforced state evolution is:

$$x_1(t) = c e^{+t}$$

Taking the limit as $t \to \infty$:

$$\lim_{t \to \infty} x_1(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} c e^{+t} = \mathbf{\infty}$$

The internal state $x_1(t)$ grows exponentially without bound. Therefore, the system is ASYMPTOTICALLY UNSTABLE!

Part C • Equivalence Theorem & Minimal Realizations

Theorem: External (BIBO) stability and internal (Asymptotic) stability are mathematically equivalent if and only if the state-space realization is MINIMAL, which requires two fundamental conditions:

  1. Complete Controllability: The Kalman Controllability Matrix: $$\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{B} & \dots & \mathbf{A}^{n-1}\mathbf{B} \end{bmatrix}$$ must possess full rank $n$. Every dynamic mode can be excited by the input.
  2. Complete Observability: The Kalman Observability Matrix: $$\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{C} \\ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A} \\ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^2 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}$$ must possess full rank $n$. Every dynamic mode is reflected in the measured output.

When a realization is minimal, no pole-zero cancellations occur. Every root of $\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$ is a pole of $G(s)$, and every pole of $G(s)$ is an eigenvalue of $\mathbf{A}$. Under minimality, BIBO stability $\iff$ Asymptotic stability. $\blacksquare$

Practical Control Engineering Warning • The Peril of Cancelling Unstable Plant Poles Students often ask: "Can we stabilize an unstable plant $G_p(s) = \frac{1}{s - 1}$ by inserting a controller zero $G_c(s) = \frac{s - 1}{s + 4}$?"
Answer: NEVER in physical hardware! In real life, exact cancellation is impossible due to component tolerances and thermal drift ($s - 0.999 \neq s - 1.000$). The closed-loop system retains an unobservable or uncontrollable unstable pole in the right-half plane. Although the output $y(t)$ may look bounded initially, the internal controller output explodes, driving actuators into violent saturation, tripping circuit breakers, or destroying power transistors!
