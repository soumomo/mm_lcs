2.1

Introduction: Physical Motivation & Limitations of Classical Methods

Verbatim exposition based on Prof. Madhubanti Maitra's Lecture Notes (Part-I, Page 2)

Theory Foundation

It has been established that the possibility of unstable operation is inherent in all feedback control systems because of the very nature of the closed-loop feedback mechanism itself. An unstable system, obviously, cannot perform the control task required of it. Therefore, while analyzing a given control system, the very first investigation that needs to be performed is whether the system is stable.

⚠️ Absolute vs. Relative Stability: Why Routh-Hurwitz Fails in Design

The determination of absolute stability of a system is a necessary but not sufficient condition. A stable system with excessively low damping is still undesirable because it exhibits severe oscillatory ringing, intolerable peak overshoot ($M_p$), and sluggish settling times ($t_s$). In an analysis and design problem, one must proceed to determine not only the absolute stability of a system, but also its relative stability.

The Routh-Hurwitz criterion gives an algebraic answer to the binary question of stability (whether roots reside in the left-half $s$-plane), but its adoption to determine relative stability is tedious, indirect, and requires repeated, blind trial-and-error calculations even for simple systems.

Consider now a design problem in which the engineer is required to achieve desired transient specifications by adjusting the location of the closed-loop poles in the complex $s$-plane through varying one or more system parameters (such as the amplifier gain $K$). The Routh criterion offers little constructive assistance in such synthesis tasks.

For determining the locations of closed-loop poles, one could resort to classical factoring of the characteristic polynomial. However, this analytical factoring approach is extremely laborious when the degree of the polynomial $n \ge 3$. Furthermore, repeated recalculations are mandatory whenever a parameter is varied during performance tuning.

🚀 The Walter R. Evans Breakthrough (1948)

A simple, powerful graphical technique introduced by W. R. Evans in 1948—known as the Root Locus Technique—solves this problem elegantly. It provides a graphical method of plotting the locus of the roots of the characteristic equation in the complex $s$-plane as a system parameter (typically open-loop gain $K$) is continuously varied over the complete range from zero to infinity ($0 \le K < \infty$).

1. Complete Dynamic View Brings into clear focus the full transient and steady-state dynamics (damping ratio $\zeta$, natural frequency $\omega_n$).
2. Parameter Sensitivity Directly provides a visual measure of the sensitivity of closed-loop roots to variations in physical system parameters.
3. Multi-Loop Scope Fully applicable to single-loop as well as complex multivariable and multi-loop feedback control architectures.
2.2

The Root Locus Concepts: Prototype Second-Order System

Mathematical derivation, 3 gain regimes, and constant settling time property (Part-I, Pages 3–4)

Derivation

To understand the fundamental concepts underlying the root locus technique, consider the canonical single-input single-output second-order feedback system shown in Figure 7.1.

Figure 7.1: Single-Loop Second-Order Feedback Control System

The open-loop transfer function is given by:

$$G(s) = \frac{K}{s(s + a)}, \quad H(s) = 1$$

where $K$ and $a$ are positive constants. The open-loop transfer function possesses two poles: one at the origin $s = 0$ and the other at $s = -a$. The closed-loop transfer function of this unity-feedback system is:

$$\frac{C(s)}{R(s)} = \frac{G(s)}{1 + G(s)} = \frac{\frac{K}{s(s+a)}}{1 + \frac{K}{s(s+a)}} = \frac{K}{s^2 + as + K} \tag{7.1}$$

The characteristic equation governing the closed-loop modes is obtained by setting the denominator equal to zero:

$$s^2 + as + K = 0 \tag{7.2}$$

Applying the quadratic formula yields the exact locations of the two closed-loop poles $s_1$ and $s_2$:

$$s_1, s_2 = -\frac{a}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 - K} \tag{7.3}$$

Since $a > 0$ and $K > 0$, the roots always possess negative real parts; hence, the system is stable for all positive values of $a$ and $K$. However, its dynamic behavior (transient response) is governed by the nature and numerical values of the roots.

Regime (1) Overdamped
$$0 \le K < \frac{a^2}{4}$$

The quantity under the radical is positive. The roots are real and distinct. At $K = 0$, $s_1 = 0$ and $s_2 = -a$ (coinciding with open-loop poles). As $K$ increases, $s_1$ moves leftward and $s_2$ moves rightward along the negative real axis towards the point $-a/2$.

Regime (2) Critically Damped
$$K = \frac{a^2}{4}$$

The radical vanishes identically. The roots are real and equal: $$s_1 = s_2 = -\frac{a}{2}$$ This point is termed the breakaway point where the two real branches collide and enter the complex plane.

Regime (3) Underdamped
$$\frac{a^2}{4} < K < \infty$$

The radical is negative. The roots are complex conjugate: $$s_{1,2} = -\frac{a}{2} \pm j\sqrt{K - \frac{a^2}{4}}$$ The real part remains strictly fixed at $\sigma = -a/2$. The locus is a vertical straight line moving toward $\pm j\infty$ as $K \to \infty$.

Figure 7.2: Root Loci of $s^2 + as + K = 0$ as a function of $K$ (Verbatim from MM Notes)
✕ Open-loop pole
Closed-loop root locus
Breakaway point ($s = -a/2$)
📌 Remarkable Dynamic Property: Constant Settling Time

For all $K > a^2/4$, the real parts of the complex roots are fixed at $\sigma = -a/2$. The 2% criterion settling time is given by: $$t_s \approx \frac{4}{\zeta \omega_n} = \frac{4}{|\sigma|} = \frac{4}{a/2} = \frac{8}{a}$$ Therefore, for any gain $K > a^2/4$, the settling time remains virtually constant! Increasing $K$ in this range only increases the damped oscillation frequency $\omega_d = \sqrt{K - a^2/4}$ and reduces the damping ratio $\zeta = \cos\theta = \frac{a}{2\sqrt{K}}$, creating higher peak overshoot without altering the settling duration.

2.3

Second-Order System with a Zero: Exact Circular Root Locus Proof

Complete trigonometric & algebraic derivation of circle equation and $\zeta_{\min}$ formula (Part-I, Pages 5–6)

JU CT 2025-26 • Q3a [8 Marks]

Consider a single-loop feedback system with an open-loop zero and two open-loop poles:

$$G(s) = K \frac{s + b}{s(s + a)}, \quad H(s) = 1 \quad (b > a > 0) \tag{7.9}$$

The open-loop transfer function has two poles at $s = 0$ and $s = -a$, and a finite zero at $s = -b$. The closed-loop characteristic equation is:

$$1 + G(s) = 0 \implies 1 + \frac{K(s + b)}{s(s + a)} = 0 \tag{7.10}$$

Applying the Evans Angle Criterion, any point $s = \sigma + j\omega$ on the root locus must satisfy:

$$\angle \left[\frac{s + b}{s(s + a)}\right] = \pm 180^\circ (2q + 1) = -\pi \tag{7.11}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation
1. Phasor angle breakdown: Substituting $s = \sigma + j\omega$ into the individual factors: $$\angle(s + b) - \angle s - \angle(s + a) = -\pi$$ $$\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega}{\sigma + b}\right) - \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega}{\sigma}\right) - \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega}{\sigma + a}\right) = -\pi$$
2. Rearranging terms: $$\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega}{\sigma}\right) + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega}{\sigma + a}\right) = \pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega}{\sigma + b}\right)$$
3. Applying the tangent identity $\tan(A+B) = \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A \tan B}$: $$\frac{\frac{\omega}{\sigma} + \frac{\omega}{\sigma + a}}{1 - \frac{\omega}{\sigma}\frac{\omega}{\sigma + a}} = \frac{\tan \pi + \frac{\omega}{\sigma + b}}{1 - \tan \pi \frac{\omega}{\sigma + b}} = \frac{0 + \frac{\omega}{\sigma + b}}{1 - 0} = \frac{\omega}{\sigma + b}$$
4. Simplifying the algebraic fractions: Combining the numerator on the LHS: $\frac{\omega(\sigma + a) + \omega\sigma}{\sigma(\sigma + a)} = \frac{\omega(2\sigma + a)}{\sigma(\sigma + a)}$. The denominator on the LHS: $\frac{\sigma(\sigma + a) - \omega^2}{\sigma(\sigma + a)}$. Hence: $$\frac{\omega(2\sigma + a)}{\sigma^2 + a\sigma - \omega^2} = \frac{\omega}{\sigma + b}$$
5. For complex roots ($\omega \neq 0$), divide both sides by $\omega$: $$\frac{2\sigma + a}{\sigma^2 + a\sigma - \omega^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma + b}$$ $$(2\sigma + a)(\sigma + b) = \sigma^2 + a\sigma - \omega^2$$ $$2\sigma^2 + (2b + a)\sigma + ab = \sigma^2 + a\sigma - \omega^2$$ $$\sigma^2 + 2b\sigma + \omega^2 = -ab$$
6. Completing the square by adding $b^2$ to both sides: $$\sigma^2 + 2b\sigma + b^2 + \omega^2 = b^2 - ab$$
$$(\sigma + b)^2 + \omega^2 = b^2 - ab \tag{7.12}$$

Equation (7.12) is the classical equation of a circle in the complex $s$-plane $(x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2 = R^2$:

Center of the Circle: $(-b, 0)$ → Exactly at the open-loop zero!
Radius of the Circle: $$R = \sqrt{b^2 - ab}$$
Figure 7.4: Circular Root Locus of $1 + \frac{K(s+b)}{s(s+a)} = 0$ with Tangent $OP$ and $\zeta_{\min}$
Minimum Damping: $\zeta_{\min} = \cos\theta = \sqrt{a/b}$
Tangent Distance: $OP = \sqrt{ab}$
Circle Radius: $R = \sqrt{b^2 - ab}$
📐 Geometric Proof: Minimum Damping Factor $\zeta_{\min} = \sqrt{a/b}$

As the gain $K$ varies along the circular trajectory, the damping ratio $\zeta = \cos\theta$ is minimized when the line from the origin makes the maximum angle $\theta$ with the negative real axis. This occurs precisely where the radial line from the origin is tangent to the circle at point $P$!

In the right-angled triangle $\triangle O C P$ formed by the origin $O$, the center $C = (-b, 0)$, and the point of tangency $P$: $$\text{Hypotenuse } OC = b, \quad \text{Radius } CP = R = \sqrt{b^2 - ab}$$ By Pythagoras' Theorem: $$OP = \sqrt{OC^2 - CP^2} = \sqrt{b^2 - (b^2 - ab)} = \sqrt{ab}$$ The minimum damping ratio is therefore: $$\zeta_{\min} = \cos \theta = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{OP}{OC} = \frac{\sqrt{ab}}{b} = \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}$$ This elegant formula is a frequent question in Jadavpur University semester examinations and viva-voce!

2.4

The Evans Angle & Magnitude Criteria

Factored transfer function representations and phasor testing (Part-I, Pages 5, 7–8)

Core Engine

The general closed-loop characteristic equation of any linear feedback control system can be expressed as:

$$1 + G(s)H(s) = 0 \iff G(s)H(s) = -1 \tag{7.6}$$

Since $s = \sigma + j\omega$ is a complex variable, the single complex equation (7.6) is equivalent to two simultaneous real conditions, known as the Evans Conditions:

Condition 1: Angle Criterion
$$\angle G(s)H(s) = \pm 180^\circ (2q + 1), \quad q = 0, 1, 2, \dots \tag{7.8}$$

Determines Geometry: A point $s_0$ in the complex $s$-plane belongs to the root locus if and only if the total angle of $G(s_0)H(s_0)$ is an odd multiple of $\pm 180^\circ$. This criterion defines the entire locus shape independently of the value of gain $K$.

Condition 2: Magnitude Criterion
$$|G(s)H(s)| = 1 \tag{7.7}$$

Determines Gain Calibration: Once a point $s_0$ is confirmed to lie on the locus by satisfying the angle criterion, the magnitude criterion is used to calculate the exact numerical value of the open-loop gain $K$ required to place the closed-loop pole at $s_0$.

Transfer Function Factored Forms

The open-loop transfer function $G(s)H(s)$ is modeled in two standard forms:

Time-Constant (Bode) Form: $$G(s)H(s) = \frac{K' \prod_{i=1}^m (\tau_{zi} s + 1)}{\prod_{j=1}^n (\tau_{pj} s + 1)} \tag{7.13}$$
Pole-Zero (Evans Root-Locus) Form: $$G(s)H(s) = \frac{K \prod_{i=1}^m (s + z_i)}{\prod_{j=1}^n (s + p_j)} \tag{7.14}$$
Interrelation between Bode Gain $K'$ and Root Locus Gain $K$: $$K = K' \frac{\prod_{j=1}^n p_j}{\prod_{i=1}^m z_i}$$ Note: For all physically realizable systems, $n \ge m$ (the number of poles $n$ is greater than or equal to the number of zeros $m$).
Figure 7.5: Determining a Point on Root Locus via Phasors (Verbatim MM Notes)
🔥 Angle Criterion Check:
$\theta_{z1} - (\theta_{p1} + \theta_{p2} + \theta_{p3}) = \pm 180^\circ(2q + 1)$
⚖️ Gain Calibration ($K$):
$K = \frac{|s_0 + p_1| \cdot |s_0 + p_2| \cdot |s_0 + p_3|}{|s_0 + z_1|}$
2.5

The 10 Master Construction Rules of Root Loci

Synthesized from MM Lecture Notes Part-I (pp. 9–13) & Part-II (pp. 1–8) with rigorous proofs

Standard Syllabus

To construct the root locus of a system whose open-loop transfer function is given in pole-zero form:

$$G(s)H(s) = \frac{K \prod_{i=1}^m (s + z_i)}{\prod_{j=1}^n (s + p_j)} \quad (n \ge m)$$

Evans formulated a set of powerful geometric and algebraic construction rules. Below is the complete mathematical treatment of each rule:

Rule 1

Symmetry of the Root Locus

Statement: The root locus is completely symmetrical with respect to the real axis ($\sigma$-axis) of the complex $s$-plane.

Proof: The coefficients of the characteristic polynomial $P(s) = \prod_{j=1}^n (s+p_j) + K \prod_{i=1}^m (s+z_i) = 0$ are real numbers for all real physical systems. By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, any non-real roots of a polynomial with real coefficients must occur in strictly complex conjugate pairs ($\sigma \pm j\omega$). Therefore, for every branch plotted in the upper half-plane ($\omega > 0$), an identical mirror-image branch must exist in the lower half-plane ($\omega < 0$).
Rule 2

Number of Branches, Starting and Terminating Points

Statement: The total number of separate branches of the root locus is equal to $N = \max(n, m) = n$ (the order of the system). As the gain parameter $K$ increases continuously from $0$ to $\infty$:

  • Each of the $n$ branches originates at $K = 0$ from an open-loop pole $-p_j$.
  • As $K \to \infty$, exactly $m$ branches terminate on the finite open-loop zeros $-z_i$.
  • The remaining $(n - m)$ branches terminate at infinity along asymptotes.
Proof: The characteristic equation can be written as:
$$\prod_{j=1}^n (s + p_j) + K \prod_{i=1}^m (s + z_i) = 0$$
When $K \to 0$, this collapses to $\prod_{j=1}^n (s + p_j) = 0$, whose roots are $s = -p_j$ ($j = 1, \dots, n$). Hence roots originate at the poles.
Dividing the characteristic equation by $K$:
$$\frac{1}{K} \prod_{j=1}^n (s + p_j) + \prod_{i=1}^m (s + z_i) = 0$$
As $K \to \infty$, the first term vanishes, leaving $\prod_{i=1}^m (s + z_i) = 0$, whose solutions are $s = -z_i$ ($i = 1, \dots, m$). For $n > m$, the magnitude criterion $|s|^{n-m} \approx K$ requires that $|s| \to \infty$ as $K \to \infty$; thus exactly $(n - m)$ branches terminate at infinity.
Rule 3

Root Locus on the Real Axis

Statement: A point $s_0$ on the real axis lies on the root locus if and only if the total number of real open-loop poles and real open-loop zeros to its right is ODD.

Figure 7.6: Phasor Angle Contributions for a Test Point $s_0$ on the Real Axis
Proof: Let $s_0$ be any point on the real axis. Consider the angles made by phasors drawn from all poles and zeros to $s_0$:
  1. Any real pole or zero lying to the left of $s_0$ makes an angle of $0^\circ$.
  2. Any real pole or zero lying to the right of $s_0$ makes an angle of $180^\circ$ (or $\pi$).
  3. Any pair of complex conjugate poles or zeros ($s_p, s_p^*$) subtends angles $+\theta$ and $-\theta$, whose sum is identically $0^\circ$ (or $360^\circ \equiv 0$).
Let $n_r$ and $m_r$ be the number of real poles and zeros to the right of $s_0$. The total phase is:
$$\angle G(s_0)H(s_0) = m_r(180^\circ) - n_r(180^\circ) = (m_r - n_r)180^\circ$$
For this angle to satisfy the Evans condition $\pm 180^\circ(2q+1)$, $(m_r - n_r)$—and hence $(n_r + m_r)$—must be an odd integer. Real segments alternate strictly between on-locus and off-locus across each real singularity.
Rule 4

Angles of the Asymptotes

Statement: The $(n - m)$ branches of the root locus that terminate on infinity do so along straight-line asymptotes making angles $\phi_A$ with the positive real axis given by:

$$\phi_A = \frac{(2q + 1)180^\circ}{n - m}, \quad q = 0, 1, 2, \dots, (n - m - 1) \tag{7.17}$$
Proof: Consider a point $s$ located at an extraordinarily large distance ($|s| \to \infty$) along a branch tending to infinity. From this remote vantage point, all the open-loop poles $-p_j$ and zeros $-z_i$ appear clustered essentially at the same location, each subtending the identical phase angle $\phi$ to $s$. The net loop phase is:
$$\angle G(s)H(s) \approx \sum_{i=1}^m \phi - \sum_{j=1}^n \phi = -(n - m)\phi$$
Equating to the Angle Criterion: $-(n - m)\phi = -(2q + 1)180^\circ \implies \phi_A = \frac{(2q + 1)180^\circ}{n - m}$. Since there are exactly $(n - m)$ such asymptotes, $q$ ranges from $0$ to $(n - m - 1)$ to produce $(n - m)$ distinct angles.
n - m = 1 180°
n - m = 2 ±90°
n - m = 3 60°, 180°, 300°
n - m = 4 45°, 135°, 225°, 315°
Rule 5

Centroid of the Asymptotes ($\sigma_A$)

Statement: The asymptotes intersect the real axis at a single point called the centroid $\sigma_A$ (denoted as $-\sigma_A$ in some textbooks):

$$\sigma_A = \frac{\sum \text{Re}(\text{poles}) - \sum \text{Re}(\text{zeros})}{n - m} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n (-p_j) - \sum_{i=1}^m (-z_i)}{n - m} \tag{7.24}$$
Rigorous Polynomial Division Proof:
Expanding the factored transfer function in powers of $s$:
$$G(s)H(s) = \frac{K [s^m + (\sum z_i)s^{m-1} + \dots]}{s^n + (\sum p_j)s^{n-1} + \dots}$$
Performing polynomial division for very large $|s|$:
$$G(s)H(s) \approx \frac{K}{s^{n-m} + [(\sum p_j) - (\sum z_i)]s^{n-m-1} + \dots}$$
For the asymptotes to radiate from a single point $\sigma_A$ on the real axis, the asymptotic behavior must match the single-point expansion:
$$\frac{K}{(s - \sigma_A)^{n-m}} = \frac{K}{s^{n-m} - (n - m)\sigma_A s^{n-m-1} + \dots}$$
Equating the coefficients of $s^{n-m-1}$ in both denominators:
$$-(n - m)\sigma_A = \sum p_j - \sum z_i = -\left[\sum (-p_j) - \sum (-z_i)\right]$$ $$\implies \sigma_A = \frac{\sum (-p_j) - \sum (-z_i)}{n - m} = \frac{\sum \text{Re}(\text{poles}) - \sum \text{Re}(\text{zeros})}{n - m}$$
Since all complex poles and zeros occur in complex conjugate pairs, their imaginary parts cancel out identically; hence $\sigma_A$ is always a strictly real quantity.
Rule 6

Breakaway and Break-in Points

Statement: The points where multiple roots of the characteristic equation occur (where branches meet and leave the real axis, or enter it) are the solutions of:

$$\frac{dK}{ds} = 0 \tag{7.28}$$
Breakaway Point (Local Maximum of K) Occurs between two open-loop poles on a valid locus segment. As $K$ increases from zero, the two real roots approach each other, collide at the breakaway point, and become complex conjugate roots. Here, $K$ reaches a local maximum along the real segment!
Break-in Point (Local Minimum of K) Occurs between two open-loop zeros (or a zero and infinity). Complex conjugate branches approach the real axis, collide at the break-in point, and separate into real branches moving toward zeros. Here, $K$ reaches a local minimum along the real segment!
⚠️ Crucial Verification Rule (MM Exam Favorite Trap)

Not all roots of $\frac{dK}{ds} = 0$ correspond to actual breakaway/break-in points! The actual breakaway points are only those roots of $\frac{dK}{ds} = 0$ that lie on a valid segment of the root locus (i.e., satisfying Rule 3 / Angle Criterion). Any root of $\frac{dK}{ds} = 0$ lying on an off-locus segment is an extraneous mathematical artifact and must be discarded.

Rule 7

Breakaway Directions / Angles of Departure from Real Axis

Statement: If $r$ branches of the root locus meet at a multiple root (breakaway point), they must approach or leave the point at equi-spaced angles given by:

$$\theta_b = \frac{\pm 180^\circ (2k + 1)}{r}, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, (r - 1)$$

For the most common case of $r = 2$ (collision of two branches), $\theta_b = \frac{\pm 180^\circ}{2} = \mathbf{\pm 90^\circ}$. Thus, the two branches always break away perpendicularly (orthogonally) to the real axis! For $r = 3$, they depart at $\pm 60^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.

Rule 8

Angle of Departure from Complex Poles & Arrival at Complex Zeros

Angle of Departure ($\phi_d$ from Complex Pole $-p_k$):
$$\phi_d = 180^\circ - \sum \theta_{pi} + \sum \theta_{zi} \tag{7.32}$$

where $\sum \theta_{pi}$ is the sum of angles from all other poles to $-p_k$, and $\sum \theta_{zi}$ is the sum of angles from all zeros to $-p_k$.

Angle of Arrival ($\phi_a$ at Complex Zero $-z_k$):
$$\phi_a = 180^\circ - \sum \theta_{zi} + \sum \theta_{pi} \tag{7.33}$$

where $\sum \theta_{zi}$ is the sum of angles from all other zeros to $-z_k$, and $\sum \theta_{pi}$ is the sum of angles from all poles to $-z_k$.

Rule 9

Intersection with the Imaginary Axis ($j\omega$-Axis Crossover)

Statement: The points where root locus branches cross the imaginary axis (marking the boundary between stability and instability) and the corresponding critical marginal gain $K_{\text{mar}}$ are found using the Routh-Hurwitz criterion:

  1. Construct the Routh array of the characteristic polynomial in terms of variable parameter $K$.
  2. Set the row of $s^1$ (or odd power) to zero to determine the critical marginal gain $K = K_{\text{mar}}$.
  3. Form the Auxiliary Equation $A(s) = 0$ from the preceding even row (typically $s^2$); its roots give the crossing frequencies: $$s = \pm j\omega_{\text{osc}}$$
Rule 10

Determination of Gain $K$ at any Desired Root Location

Statement: From the Magnitude Criterion, the open-loop gain $K$ corresponding to any specific closed-loop pole $s_0$ located on the root locus is given by:

$$K = \frac{\prod_{j=1}^n |s_0 + p_j|}{\prod_{i=1}^m |s_0 + z_i|} = \frac{\text{Product of phasor lengths from } s_0 \text{ to open-loop poles}}{\text{Product of phasor lengths from } s_0 \text{ to open-loop zeros}} \tag{7.34}$$

Scale Rule: When measuring phasor lengths directly from graph paper, ensure both the real and imaginary axes share the exact same physical scale (e.g. $1\text{ cm} = 1\text{ unit}$).

2.6

Breakaway and Break-in Points: Analytical & Graphical Methods

Evaluation techniques, real-axis extrema, and complex breakaway points (Part-II, pp. 2–5)

Deep Dive

The evaluation of the breakaway points from $\frac{dK}{ds} = 0$ is one of the most critical steps in root locus sketching. In her lectures, Prof. Madhubanti Maitra highlights two distinct analytical and physical approaches:

Method 1: Direct Algebraic Differentiation

Express gain $K$ explicitly as a rational function of $s$ from the characteristic equation $B(s) + K A(s) = 0 \implies K = -B(s)/A(s)$. Compute $\frac{dK}{ds} = -\frac{B'(s)A(s) - B(s)A'(s)}{A(s)^2} = 0$, which simplifies to: $$B'(s)A(s) - B(s)A'(s) = 0$$ Find all roots of this polynomial and test each against Rule 3 (must lie on an on-locus segment).

Method 2: Physical / Graphical Extrema Approach

Along the real axis ($s = \sigma$), the condition $\frac{dK}{d\sigma} = 0$ signifies an extremum of gain $K$:

  • Breakaway: Two poles meet → $K$ increases from $0$ to a local maximum at the breakaway point!
  • Break-in: Branches enter real axis towards zeros → $K$ decreases to a local minimum at the break-in point!
Figure 7.10: Graphical Plot of $K$ vs $\sigma$ on Real Axis for $1 + \frac{K}{s(s+1)(s+2)} = 0$ (MM Notes)

Detailed Case Study: Break-in Point with Complex Poles Example 7.3

Consider the closed-loop system: $$1 + \frac{K(s + 2)}{s^2 + 2s + 2} = 0$$ The open-loop poles are at $s = -1 \pm j1$ and a finite zero is at $s = -2$.

$$K = -\frac{s^2 + 2s + 2}{s + 2} \implies \frac{dK}{ds} = -\frac{(2s + 2)(s + 2) - (s^2 + 2s + 2)}{(s + 2)^2} = -\frac{s^2 + 4s + 2}{(s + 2)^2} = 0$$
Roots of $s^2 + 4s + 2 = 0$:
$$s_{1,2} = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 8}}{2} = -2 \pm \sqrt{2} = \mathbf{-0.586} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{-3.414}$$
Angle Criterion Segregation:
• Point $s = -0.586$ has $0$ real poles/zeros to its right $\implies$ Even (0) $\implies$ Off root locus! Discarded!
• Point $s = -3.414$ has $1$ real zero (at $-2$) to its right $\implies$ Odd (1) $\implies$ Valid Break-in Point!
At $s = -3.414$, the gain reaches a local minimum $K = 4.828$. The branches depart the complex poles at $\phi_d = 135^\circ$, curve towards the real axis, enter at $-3.414$, and split along the real axis.
Figure 7.11: Root Locus Plot of $1 + \frac{K(s+2)}{s^2 + 2s + 2} = 0$ with Break-in Point (MM Notes)
Advanced

Complex Breakaway Points: Example 7.4

Breakaway points are not restricted to the real axis; systems with higher degree can possess complex breakaway points:

"Explain the significance of the equation $ rac{dK}{ds} = 0$ on the real axis. [06 Marks]"

1. Mathematical Significance: Condition for Multiple (Repeated) Closed-Loop Roots

Let the open-loop transfer function be (s)H(s) = rac{A(s)}{B(s)}$, where (s)$ and (s)$ are polynomials in $. The closed-loop characteristic equation is: 64124F(s, K) = B(s) + K A(s) = 0 \implies K(s) = - rac{B(s)}{A(s)}64124 Differentiating with respect to $ using the quotient rule: 64124 rac{dK}{ds} = - rac{B'(s)A(s) - B(s)A'(s)}{[A(s)]^2} = 0 \iff B'(s)A(s) - B(s)A'(s) = 064124 Now, consider the condition for a polynomial (s)$ to have a repeated (multiple) root of multiplicity $\ge 2$: both the function and its first derivative must vanish simultaneously, i.e., (s) = 0$ and $ rac{\partial F}{\partial s} = 0$: 64124 rac{\partial F}{\partial s} = B'(s) + K A'(s) = 0 \implies K = - rac{B'(s)}{A'(s)}64124 Equating the two expressions for $ gives precisely: 64124- rac{B(s)}{A(s)} = - rac{B'(s)}{A'(s)} \implies B'(s)A(s) - B(s)A'(s) = 064124 Conclusion: The equation $ rac{dK}{ds} = 0$ is the exact analytical condition for the closed-loop system to have coincident (multiple) roots in the complex 569Xplane.

2. Physical & Geometric Meaning on the Real Axis

When restricted to the real axis ( = \sigma$), the equation (\sigma) = - rac{B(\sigma)}{A(\sigma)}$ defines a real scalar profile. The extrema ($ rac{dK}{d\sigma} = 0$) represent two distinct physical phenomena:

Breakaway Point (Local Maximum)

Occurs on a real-axis segment bounded by two open-loop poles. As gain $ increases from zsh$, two real roots travel towards each other. At $ rac{dK}{d\sigma} = 0$ with $ rac{d^2K}{d\sigma^2} < 0$, gain $ achieves its local maximum on that segment. For > K_{\max}$, the roots cannot remain on the real axis; they collide and break away into the complex plane as complex conjugate pairs.

Break-in Point (Local Minimum)

Occurs on a real-axis segment bounded by two open-loop zeros (or a zero and 569X\infty$). Two complex branches travel toward the real axis. At $ rac{dK}{d\sigma} = 0$ with $ rac{d^2K}{d\sigma^2} > 0$, gain $ reaches its local minimum. For higher gains, the branches enter the real axis and split into two distinct real roots moving toward the zeros.

3. Root Sensitivity Significance

The sensitivity of a closed-loop root location $ with respect to gain $ is defined by: 64124S_K^s = rac{ds}{dK} = rac{1}{ rac{dK}{ds}}64124 At any point where $ rac{dK}{ds} = 0$, the root sensitivity becomes infinite: 64124\lim_{ rac{dK}{ds} o 0} \left| rac{ds}{dK} ight| = \infty64124 Geometric consequence: An infinitesimal increment in gain $\Delta K$ produces an infinitely rapid rate of displacement of the roots, causing the locus branches to diverge abruptly at perpendicular angles ($\pm 90^\circ$ for two colliding branches, or $\pm rac{180^\circ}{n}$ for $ branches).

4. Necessary vs. Sufficient Criterion (The Examiner Trap)

$ rac{dK}{ds} = 0$ is a necessary mathematical condition, but not sufficient by itself. A real root $\sigma$ of $ rac{dK}{ds} = 0$ corresponds to a true physical breakaway/break-in point if and only if it satisfies both tests:

  • Locus Segment Test: $\sigma$ must lie on an allowed real-axis segment of the root locus (odd number of real poles and zeros to its right).
  • Gain Positivity Test: The gain evaluated at that coordinate must be strictly positive: (\sigma) = - rac{B(\sigma)}{A(\sigma)} > 0$.

Roots failing either condition are extraneous algebraic artifacts and must be rejected.

Effects of Adding Poles & Zeros • $0^\circ$ Root Loci

Dynamic compensation mechanisms and positive feedback loci

Synthesis
1. Effect of Adding an Open-Loop Pole
  • Pulls Root Locus Rightward: Moves locus branches toward the imaginary axis and into the right-half $s$-plane.
  • Reduces Stability Margin: The critical gain $K_{\text{mar}}$ decreases, narrowing the stable operating range.
  • Increases Settling Time: Slower transient decay due to smaller magnitude of real parts $|\sigma|$.
  • Increases Number of Asymptotes: As $(n - m)$ increases by 1, asymptotic angles rotate toward the imaginary axis.
2. Effect of Adding an Open-Loop Zero
  • Pulls Root Locus Leftward: Bends branches away from the $j\omega$-axis deeper into the stable left-half $s$-plane.
  • Enhances Relative Stability: Increases damping ratio $\zeta$ and reduces peak overshoot $M_p$.
  • Speeds Up Response: Decreases settling time $t_s$ (the foundational principle of Lead Compensator and PD control).
  • Reduces Number of Asymptotes: By reducing $(n - m)$, branches are anchored by finite zeros rather than escaping to infinity.
